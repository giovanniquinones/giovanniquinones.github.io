During my latest trip to Bolivia I couldn't help but nnotice one particular thing: Dogs roam freely in  public.
Growing up I was used to seeing dogs on the streets by themselves and never thought much of it. After returning after 
7 years, I noticed just how different our treatment of dogs is copared to the United States. 

I have a dog in the United STates called Pacha. Here, it;s considered irresponsible to walk your dog without a leash 
because it could bite someone, run into the street. This is predicated on the basis that dogs are unpredictable and we, 
as humans and their owners, are liable for their behavior. Additionally, there is the empathetic side of the eqation, 
where it;s considered careless and inhumane to let your dog outside without supervision.

If you walk through any town or even major cities in Bolivia, you will see a lot of dogs on the street. I also believe this is true for most latin-american countries as well. Most of the time they are simply laying down on the sidewak resting. Sometimes, you can see them walking for blocks and crossing streets as if they had a specific destination. In the poorer neighborhoods, you can see entire gangs of dogs walking around together, intimidating passerby.

## Why?

A lot of owners keep their dogs outside intentionally. I believe this is rooted on the old//traditional view that dogs are guardian 
of the house and therefore mmust stay outside to alert of any intruder. I believe in more developed countries like the US, this is no longer the image  we have of dosgs. I certainly do not expect Pacha to protect me and my girlfriend if someone breaks in. Howeever, in Bolivia, this mentality is still very common, where people perceive dogs a lot more like an employee than as a companion. Of course this is not true for everyone. A lot of people across multiple age groups treat their pets with a lot more consideration as if they were a member of the family or even more touching - their only companion. 

Many families may not necessarily view their dog as the guardian but still keep their dogs outside for a different reason. Mosst houses in Bolivia are built wall-to-wall. This means that most houses do not have a patio or yard at all. While in the US it's common
to have a lawn, a yard or some sort of outdoor area, in latin-america most families maximize their living space. Because of safety, it is not common to have a front or back patio, espacialyly for the lower income families where their housing area is already very limited. Dogs are also considered ;to dirty; to be kept inside their living space, so they default for lieaving them outside. 

In some more rare cases, dogs imply find the way to go out. Just like rebelious teenagers, my dogs at my paren;t house simply figure out how to squueze out of our old gate to go into the streets.

## A canon event 

The most common breakfast and dinner in Bolivia is to simply drink your hot drink of choice (coffee, tea, chocolate) with bread. As part of our daily routine, we go to the neighborhod store to buy bread every single day (Our bread doesn't have the amount of preservatives the american bread has, so we have to buyt it fresh dialy). As a kid, the short trip to the store was often more adventerous than it needed to be, because I had to dodge some of the street dogs that were quite aggresive. Most of them only barked at me, which, as a kid is already frightening. But some of them would even go as far as to chase me down. The 'ole-reliable' trick was to 'pretend throw' a rock at them. As I grew older and bigger, I learned to simply walk past and ignore them, no manner how close they get to you. You also learn to face them head on and see who intimated who.

When you ride pass a dog on your bike, they will often run after you. You learn a lot about the personality of indivdual dogs based on how far and how aggresively they chase after you. You can tell some of them do it out of habit or instinct, and some of them really want you to stay away from their territory.
